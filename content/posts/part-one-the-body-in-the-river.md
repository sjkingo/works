The telephone rang at eleven minutes past nine on a Tuesday morning, which was not, in Cole's experience, a time that telephones rang with good news. He was filling out a signals intake form, the third of the morning, a task so routine his hand had stopped consulting his brain about it some time around the second paragraph. He let the phone ring twice more before he answered it. Old habit. Answering too quickly told the caller you'd been waiting, and Cole had not been waiting for anything in a long time.

"Cole"

"Raymond" Ashford's voice. Even, unhurried, pitched at the register it always found when things were serious. Ashford never raised his voice. He lowered it. It was one of the many things about Ashford that Cole had once admired and now merely noted. "I need you upstairs"

"When?"

"Now would be agreeable"

Cole set down his pen. He looked at the intake form and at the stack beside it, and out the window to London doing what London always did, which was to proceed as though nothing that happened inside these walls was of any consequence to the world beyond them. London, in Cole's assessment, was not entirely wrong.

He took the stairs. The lift worked, most days, but he preferred the stairs because they told him things the lift did not: who was on which floor, which doors were open, where conversations stopped as he passed. Not that anyone stopped conversations when Cole passed anymore. He had been on the signals desk for three years. The people who had once stopped talking in his presence had been reassigned, retired, or had simply forgotten he existed, which in the Service amounted to the same thing.

Ashford's office was on the fourth floor, the corner that caught the morning light when there was morning light to catch, which in November there was not. The door was open. Cole knocked on the frame anyway. It was a habit he had never broken, knocking on open doors, and Ashford had never told him to stop. There were pleasantries between them that predated their current arrangement, pleasantries that both men maintained the way one maintains a fence between your neighbour: because without it the boundary is unclear.

"Sit down, Raymond. How are you keeping?"

The question was not pleasantry. It was Ashford. He asked it of everyone, always had: the desk officers, the signals clerks, the men returning from postings that had largely been forgotten about. A small procedural kindness, delivered with the same professional composure he applied to everything else. Cole had once found it genuine - he now found it habitual.

"Well enough"

Cole sat. Ashford was behind his desk in the posture Cole remembered from a decade of briefings: upright, hands flat, the Telegraph folded and set aside. Ashford dressed the way certain men of his generation dressed, as though the act of putting on a suit was an obligation rather than a personal choice. He was fifty-eight years old and looked, as he had always looked, like a man for whom the institution had been specifically designed.

"An asset designated CARDINAL has been found dead in the Vltava."

Cole said nothing. He knew what CARDINAL was. He knew *who* CARDINAL was. He had recruited CARDINAL himself in a cafe in Brno in 1972, over coffee so bad it tasted as though the beans had been boiled twice and then punished for it. He had run CARDINAL for eleven years. He had then handed him to his successor on the Central Europe Desk when the Service moved Cole sideways and downward to the place where men were moved when they had become inconvenient without becoming quite dispensable.

"Official Czech position," Ashford continued, "is that a drunk fell from the a bridge. Unfortunate. Not unusual. The Vltava is accommodating in that regard."

"Kovář didn't drink"

"No"

"He didn't drink, Peter. Not socially. Not privately. I ran him for eleven years and never once saw him take anything stronger than that coffee in Brno, which in fairness should have qualified as an industrial solvent."

"I'm aware of his habits, Raymond"

Cole looked at the wall behind Ashford's head. There was a print of something, sheep in a field, the kind of thing the Service bought in bulk from a supplier and distributed to offices above a certain grade. The sheep were indifferent. Cole found this appropriate.

"How did they find him?"

"The body surfaced and a fisherman, though God knows what anyone expects to catch in the Vltava in November, found him. The identification was confirmed through the residency's back channel. Dental records. The face was not in a condition to be useful."

Cole absorbed this without expression. He had been absorbing things without expression for thirty years.

"There was an exfiltration window," Ashford said. "Opened seventy-two hours before the body was found. Thirty-six-hour operational window. Kovář was to be pulled and brought to Vienna."

"Who held the window?"

Ashford opened a drawer. He removed a single sheet of paper and placed it on the desk between them. Four names, typewritten. Cole took it and read the names twice, which was once more than he needed. He folded it along the crease already in it and put it in his inside pocket.

"Four people," Ashford said. "Myself. Fenton, your successor on the Desk. Hartley in Signals. Briggs, the duty clerk. No one else had operational knowledge of the window."

"And the window leaked."

"Something leaked. The StB were waiting. They had Kovář before the window was three hours old."

Cole looked at the sheep again. They remained indifferent.

"You want me to go."

"I want you to go to Prague under commercial cover. Import consult. The cover firm is established, the documentation will be ready by Thursday. You will liaise with the residency only as necessary and you will tell them nothing about the nature of your inquiry. You will find the leak. You will return. You will report to me and to no one else."

"And if the leak is on that list?"

Ashford's expression did not change. "Then you will find it, Raymond. That is what you do. That is what you have always done."

Cole stood. He buttoned his jacket. The muscles and habits of a career he had thought finished were tightening again, and his body remembered what his mind had been trying to forget for three years.

He almost refused. The refusal was there, a complete sentence, sitting behind his teeth like a coin he had been carrying and had forgotten to spend. He did not refuse. Not because of duty. Not because of Ashford. Because Martin Kovář had been found in the Vltava with a face the river had rendered useless, and Kovář had not drunk, and Kovář had been precise, and Kovář had trusted the Service with his life, and the Service had held that trust the way it held everything: firmly, until it didn't.

"Thursday, then" Cole said.

"Thursday. The documentation will be at your flat by Wednesday evening by courier"

Cole went back down the stairs. Four flights. The intake forms were where he had left them. The pen was where he had left it. Nothing in the room had changed. He picked up the pen and put it down again with more force than was necessary, which surprised him, because Cole did not do things with more force than was necessary. He left the form unfinished and sat there for twenty minutes doing nothing, which was not something he could account for and did not try to.

---

Nineteen seventy-two. Cole had been sent to Czechoslovakia on a routine asset-spotting tour, the kind of trip that usually produced nothing except bad meals and the feeling of being watched by everyone who was also being watched by everyone else. He had been in the cafe that day because his train was delayed, which in Czechoslovakia was about as common as the cold.

Kovář had been at the next table. He was reading a newspaper and drinking coffee and doing both with the particular interest of a man who was neither reading nor enjoying the coffee. It was a clever cover, one that allowed men like Kovář and Cole to go unnoticed. Cole noticed.

They had spoken because the coffee was bad enough to constitute a shared grievance. The conversation that followed was, on its surface, about nothing: the futility of forms, the weight of bureaucracies, the way systems designed to serve people inevitably required those people to serve the system instead. It was the kind of conversation that happened in cafes across Eastern Europe between men who were tired, though what they were tired of was never stated.

Cole had known within eight minutes that Kovář was StB. The shoes were wrong. Not bad, not good, but simply not the shoes of the low-ranking administrator Kovář claimed to be. And the way he held his newspaper, folded in quarters, reading the columns that carried the administrative appointments rather than the news, told Cole that this was a man who understood the architecture of power at a level that clerks did not reach.

The recruitment had taken four meetings over six weeks. There was no money discussed. There was no ideology offered. Cole did not promise liberation or justice or the triumph of Western democracy, because he did not believe in any of those things with sufficient conviction to offer them to another man. What he offered, without stating it, was a channel. A way for the intelligence Kovář collected in the normal course of his work to go somewhere that was not the filing cabinet in a Prague office where it would be read by men who used it to tighten the machine another quarter-turn.

Kovář accepted for reasons he never explained and Cole never asked. At the end of that first meeting, as they stood to leave, Kovář had brushed something from the lapel of Cole's coat. A thread, or a piece of lint, or nothing at all. It was a small, reflexive gesture, and it was so unexpected from a man Cole had known for ninety minutes that Cole had not known what to do with it. He had carried it for eleven years. He carried it now, at Heathrow, waiting for a flight to the city where Kovář had died. It was not useful. It explained nothing. It was simply the thing Cole remembered when he did not mean to remember anything.

Eleven years. Reliable, precise, cautious to the point of invisibility. The best asset Cole ever ran. The best asset, he suspected, the Service had ever had in the Central European section, though the Service would never have said so. The Service did not rank its assets. Ranking implied attachment, and attachment was not something the Service encouraged in any direction.

---

Prague in November was exactly what he remembered. Stone cold and silent, the kind of silence a city learns when it has been watching itself for thirty years. The Vltava was grey and fast below the bridges, moving with the indifference of a river that had been used to dispose of inconvenient truths before and would be used again.

Cole took a taxi from the airport. The driver did not speak. Cole did not encourage him to. The city moved past the window in slabs of grey and brown, punctuated by the red of a tram, the white of a police car, the institutional green that Communist countries applied to buildings to indicate the state had been there and marked them as its own.

The hotel was a commercial establishment used by Western businessmen who came to Prague for reasons the state pretended to believe and the state's intelligence apparatus did not. Cole checked in under his cover name, which was Henderson, and carried his bag to a room on the third floor that smelled of furniture polish and damp and something else, something Cole recognised from every hotel room he had ever occupied east of Vienna. The room knew it was listened to. You could feel it in the walls.

He did not sweep the room for listening devices. Henderson the import consultant would not know how to sweep a room, and Cole was Henderson until he was not. He unpacked and hung his jacket and washed his face and stood at the window, doing each thing slowly, the way a man does things when he is putting off the moment he will have to think.

The Vltava was there, visible between buildings, a grey line moving through the grey city. Somewhere in the last week, Martin Kovář's body had moved along that line, turning slowly in water that was cold enough to preserve and fast enough to disfigure. Cole looked at the river and thought about the cafe in Brno and the bad coffee and the conversation about paperwork and the eleven years that had followed, years in which Cole had asked a man to risk his life for a country that was not his own and an institution that would deny his existence if it became convenient to do so. Kovář had said yes. He had kept saying yes, every scheduled contact, every dead drop, every piece of intelligence folded into the mechanism that Cole maintained with the precision of a watchmaker and the emotional investment of a plumber. And now the river had him.

Cole did not sleep that night. He sat in the chair by the window and watched the city settle into darkness, which it did not so much fall into as reveal: the darkness had always been there, underneath the thin November light, waiting. The streetlamps came on, sulphurous and weak. A tram passed. Then another. Then silence.

He took out the sheet Ashford had given him and looked at the four names by the light of the desk lamp. Fenton. Hartley. Briggs. Ashford. Four people who knew about the exfiltration window. Four people, one of whom had given that knowledge, directly or indirectly, to the StB, who had used it to be waiting when Kovář tried to leave.

The investigation was bounded. Four names on a page. Cole put the page away and looked at the river and began the work.

---

The residency was on the second floor of a building above a shop that sold paper and envelopes and rubber stamps to the citizens of a city that required paperwork the way other cities required oxygen. Cole went there on his second morning. He walked, because walking told you things that taxis didn't: where the surveillance cameras were, which streets had checkpoints, where the police stood and where they stood only sometimes, which was more useful than where they stood always.

The residency officer was a man named Collis who still had the undamaged look of someone the institution hadn't got around to disappointing yet. Collis was helpful. Collis was professional. Collis asked no questions about why a signals analyst from London had been sent to Prague under commercial cover with instructions to review the file of a dead asset.

"CARDINAL's operational file" Cole said. "Everything from the last six months. Communications, movement logs, contact records. The residency's observations, if you keep those."

"We keep them," Collis said. "I'll need authorisation."

"You'll find it on your desk by this afternoon. Ashford will cable."

"And Collis"

"Yes?"

"The cable will come through Ashford's personal channel. Not the Desk. Not the general signals traffic. If anyone other than Ashford asks what I'm doing here, the answer is that Henderson is reviewing import licensing procedures for a firm in Coventry and you have never heard of him."

Collis looked at Cole for a moment longer than was comfortable. "Understood."

Cole left. He spent the afternoon in his hotel room with the curtains half-drawn, reading the file Collis delivered by hand in a leather attaché case that had seen better decades. He read it once, completely, the way one reads a novel, for shape and flow and the things that are present in the spaces between facts. Then he read it again, with a pencil, and this time he was not reading. He was listening.

Kovář's last six months. Contact reports: five scheduled, three made, two missed. The missed contacts were noted in the file with the brevity the Service applied to all failures: "CARDINAL did not present at the designated time. No signal received. Standing protocols observed. Next contact per schedule." Cole read these entries three times. Two missed contacts in the final eight weeks. The file treated them as anomalies. Cole treated them as the first piece of the story that mattered.

Movement logs, compiled from the residency's limited observation capacity and supplemented by what the Service called "collateral reporting," which meant intelligence purchased from sources who were themselves probably compromised: Kovář had continued his routine, which was the routine of a careful man. Work, home the occasional tram to a park where he sat on a bench and read a book. Nothing in the movement logs suggested panic. Nothing suggested flight. Nothing suggested a man who knew he was about to die.

But Kovář had known. Cole was certain of this. Eleven years of running a man teaches you how he thinks, and Kovář would have felt the surveillance. Not seen it. Felt it. A man who has lived his entire adult life inside a surveillance state develops a sense for the weight of attention. Kovář had felt it and he had not run. He had missed two scheduled contacts and Cole, sitting in his hotel room in Prague with the file on his knees and the river outside the window, began to understand why.

He opened his notebook. He wrote the date. He wrote: *Two missed contacts. Not caution. Protection.* He underlined the second word and closed the notebook and looked at the river and did not move for a long time.

---

The safe house was on a narrow street in a district that looked the way all Prague districts looked in November: grey, watched, and old. The building was five storeys. The flat was on the third floor. It smelled of damp plaster and boiled cabbage and time. Rooms like this always smelled of time. They were not lived in. There was a desk, a bed, a chair, a window that faced a small courtyard. Cole set his bag on the bed and opened the window and looked at the courtyard. Quiet and peaceful. You could hear any footsteps approaching, which was something that a good intelligence officer would know to listen for.

Then he began. He unpacked his materials: the file, his notebook, two pencils and a knife for sharpening them, and the sheet of paper with four names. He laid them on the desk the way he always laid them, and the familiar arrangement of it was, in its small way, the closest thing to comfort he was likely to find in Prague.

He worked through the evening. The light outside went from grey to dark. He did not turn on the overhead light, using instead the desk lamp, which cast a circle of yellow on the papers and left the rest of the room in shadow. At some point he stopped working and made instant coffee from the supplies left in the flat, which were minimal and stale, and stood at the window drinking it. The courtyard was dark. Still quiet and peaceful. The building opposite was dark too, except for one window on the fourth floor where a light burned. As Cole watched, a shape moved across the lit window. Then the light went out.

Cole noted this. He noted everything. He finished his coffee and went back to the desk and continued working.

The investigation was bounded. Four names on a page. By midnight, Cole had begun the process of elimination. He opened his notebook and began to build the matrix: who knew what, when they knew it, how the knowledge moved, where the channels intersected, and where, in the exfiltration window, a crack existed through which information could flow from the Service to the StB and a man could end up in the Vltava.

Cole worked. At some point he stopped and looked at the matrix and realised that the shape of the investigation was wrong. He did not yet know how. He did not yet know why. But the four names on the page were not going to be enough, and somewhere beneath what Ashford had given him, something else was moving, and Cole could feel it the way Kovář must have felt it, and he went back to work.

Something wasn't adding up.

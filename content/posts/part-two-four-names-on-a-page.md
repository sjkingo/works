Cole eliminated Briggs first, because Briggs was the easiest and because Cole believed in clearing the obvious before attending to the difficult. It was the principle by which he had conducted every investigation in twenty years and it had not yet failed him, which was not the same as saying it had always been correct. It meant only that the failures had not been visible, and in the Service, invisibility was indistinguishable from success.

Briggs was the duty clerk. Twenty-six years old, three years in post, vetted to a standard that would not have troubled a determined adversary but would have caught an obvious one. Cole reviewed his access logs from the London registry, sent via Ashford's personal channel in a cable that arrived at the residency at six in the morning and was in Cole's hands by seven. Briggs had logged the exfiltration window at 14:07 on the day it was authorised. He had processed the supporting paperwork. He had filed the distribution copy. He had gone home at half past five and, according to the notes that Ashford had appended without being asked, had spent the evening in a pub with two friends from school, neither of whom had any connection to any intelligence service of any nation.

His bank statements, which Ashford had also appended without being asked to, showed nothing out of the ordinary. Ashford had also added a margin note: "Briggs father unwell. Consideration re: interview timing appreciated." It was the kind of thing Ashford did. He remembered people's fathers. He remembered birthdays and the names of children and the small facts that most senior officers treated as noise. Cole had once thought this was Ashford's strength. He now thought it was Ashford's habit, and he was no longer certain there was a difference. There was nothing here that meant anything.

Cole crossed Briggs off the list in his notebook. Not because Briggs was innocent in any absolute sense, but because if Briggs was the leak, the leak was so clean that no investigation would find it, and Cole did not believe the StB recruited duty clerks with that level of craft. The StB recruited where there was leverage, and Briggs had nothing to leverage: no family in hostile territory, no debts beyond the ordinary, no attachments that could be turned into obligations. He was the wrong shape.

Hartley was next. Signals officer. Forty-one. Hartley's access to the exfiltration window was technical: he had processed the signal authorising the operational window and had transmitted the confirmation to the Prague residency through the Service's encrypted channel. He had not seen the signal's content in full. He had seen the routing headers and the classification markers, which told him that something operational was moving through Central Europe but not what, and certainly not who.

Cole spent half a day on Hartley. He reviewed the signals traffic for the relevant period, looking for the specific pattern of a man who copies what he transmits: a delay in processing, a second access to the same file, a routing anomaly that would indicate the signal had been read twice. There was nothing. Hartley had processed the signal cleanly and moved on to the next item in his queue, which was an intercept summary from the Berlin station that nobody in London would read for another three days.

Hartley came off the list. Cole drew a line through his name and moved on. Two down. Two left. The list was getting shorter and Cole was not getting closer, and he knew it.

Fenton was harder. Fenton was Cole's successor on the Central Europe Desk, appointed after Cole's reassignment. Mid-thirties. Ambitious in the way that the Service rewarded: quietly, structurally, without the displays of competence that older institutions distrusted. Fenton knew CARDINAL's real name. Fenton knew the exfiltration plan. Fenton had, in the normal course of his duties, full operational access to everything that touched the Central Europe Desk, including the movements of assets, the schedules of contacts, and the architecture of the networks that Cole had built over eleven years.

Cole reviewed Fenton's cables. He reviewed his access logs. He reviewed, with the thoroughness that had made him the best internal investigator the Service had produced and the least popular man at any gathering that included people he had investigated, every document Fenton had touched in the six months preceding Kovář's death. He found nothing that a competent desk officer would not have touched. He found nothing missing that a competent desk officer would not have missed. He found, in short, the professional footprint of a man doing his job, which was either the truth or the most convincing kind of cover.

On the fourth day in Prague, sitting at his desk in the safe house with the courtyard dark outside and the desk lamp casting its yellow circle, Cole crossed Fenton off the list. The pencil line was heavier than the others. Not because Cole was less certain. Because Fenton left him with Ashford, and Ashford was the name he had been avoiding the way a man avoids a room in which he knows something unpleasant is waiting.

---

He went to the StB administrative offices on a Friday. Not the main building which would have been suicide, but a satellite office that handled the clerical bore of state security: filing, indexing, the vast bureaucracy of a surveillance state that documented everything and understood almost nothing. Cole went as Henderson the import consultant, carrying a briefcase containing documents that were entirely genuine and entirely irrelevant, a cover story about customs licensing procedures that he delivered to a bored clerk at the front desk.

The clerk directed him to a waiting area. Cole waited. Waiting was something he did well. He had once waited for eleven hours in a car park in East Berlin for a source who never came, and had used the time to memorise the registration plates of every vehicle that entered and left, a feat of attention that had later identified a BND officer operating under West German diplomatic cover, which had earned Cole a commendation he had never read and a reputation he had never wanted.

He was not here for customs licensing. He was here because Kovář had worked in this building, at a desk on the second floor, in a room shared with four other low-ranking StB administrators whose function was to file, to index, and to keep the vast machine of Czechoslovak state security supplied with the paper it consumed in quantities that would have impressed a medieval monastery. Kovář's desk had been cleared, according to Collis, the week after his death. His personal effects had been boxed and stored. His files had been redistributed. The StB had processed Kovář's death with the same institutional efficiency it applied to everything: completely, routinely, and without sentiment.

But Cole knew something the StB did not know: Kovář kept things. Not operational materials, not intelligence in any form that a search would identify. Kovář kept the architecture. The patterns. The names of people who moved through his professional world, filed in a system of his own devising, because Kovář understood that in a surveillance state, the most dangerous information is not what is hidden but what is overlooked. He kept it in a place that was plain and therefore, in a system designed to find the sophisticated, invisible.

Cole found the desk. Second floor, third room on the left, by the window. It had been reassigned to a young officer who was not present, his lunch tray suggesting he would return soon. Cole worked quickly. The desk was metal, standard issue, with three drawers on the right side and a shallow centre drawer that contained pens, paperclips, and a rubber stamp bearing the departmental seal. He opened the bottom drawer. Files, neatly arranged. He lifted them and felt the bottom.

The false bottom was so elementary it was almost heartbreaking. A piece of cardboard, cut to size, resting on the metal base with a gap of perhaps three millimetres. Kovář had not glued it. He had not secured it. He had simply placed it there and trusted it to the principle that had governed his entire professional life: that the most effective concealment is the absence of anything worth concealing.

Cole lifted the cardboard. Beneath it was a single sheet of paper. He took it, replaced the cardboard, replaced the files, closed the drawer, and left the building through the same door he had entered, passing the bored clerk who did not look up.

Outside, in the cold, on a street where the tram tracks ran down the centre of the road and the buildings were the colour of old teeth, Cole unfolded the paper. His hands were not steady. This bothered him. His hands were always steady. They had been steady in East Berlin and in a hotel room in Bucharest where a source had been dying on the bathroom floor, and in Ashford's office that morning in London. They were not steady now. He held the paper and waited for the steadiness to return and it did, after a few seconds, but the few seconds stayed with him. Kovář's handwriting. He had seen it on a hundred intelligence reports and it had never meant anything except information. It meant something now. It meant a dead man had sat at this desk and written these names and put the paper under a piece of cardboard and gone to work and eventually gone to the Vltava, and Cole was holding the last thing Kovář had hidden, and his hands had not been steady.

Seven names. Handwritten in Kovář's small, precise script. Six of them Cole recognised immediately: StB surveillance officers, the men who had been assigned to watch Kovář in his final months. Kovář had identified his own watchers. Of course he had. He had been StB himself. He knew the patterns, the rhythms, the bureaucratic choreography of a surveillance operation mounted from inside the same organisation in which the target worked. He had identified six of them, written their names in a hand that did not tremble, and filed the list in a desk drawer with a false bottom so simple it was either genius or despair.

The seventh name was not like the others. It was not a name at all. It was a designation: LAZARUS. Written in a format Cole did not recognise. Not StB standard, where designations followed a particular alphanumeric convention. Not Service registry, which used its own system, familiar to Cole from two decades of reading files. LAZARUS was in a notation that belonged to neither system, and beside it, in Kovář's hand, so small it might have been a slip of the pen except that Kovář did not make slips of the pen, was a question mark.

Cole stared at the list. He stood on the street in the cold and stared at the seventh name and the question mark and tried to place the notation and could not. He folded the paper and put it in his inside pocket, the same pocket where Ashford's list of four names had rested since London. 

---

He ran LAZARUS through every database available to him. This was not many. In Prague, operating under commercial cover with a residency that knew him only as a man Ashford had sent, Cole's access was limited to what Collis could provide and what Ashford would cable. He sent a request through the personal channel: "Require identification of designation LAZARUS. Non-standard format. Not Service registry. Not StB convention. Priority."

Ashford's reply came eighteen hours later. Brief, as Ashford's replies always were: "LAZARUS not found in central registry. No historical match. Designation format unrecognised. Will run extended search. Advise patience."

Cole did not advise patience. Cole did not advise anything. He put the cable in his pocket with the two lists and sat at his desk in the safe house and looked at the wall and thought about LAZARUS.

The question mark. Kovář had placed it there with the deliberation of a man who measured every mark he made, because every mark was a risk, and every risk was weighed against the possibility that this piece of paper would be the last thing he ever wrote. LAZARUS was not confirmed. LAZARUS was suspected. Kovář had noticed someone in his surveillance web who did not fit the pattern of the other six, and he had not been certain what that person was. He had written the designation in a format that was not StB and was not Service and was not anything Cole could identify.

Cole thought about LAZARUS at night. He thought about LAZARUS on the tram. He thought about LAZARUS the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: not by choice but because the gap was there and the gap demanded attention.

---

Cole cabled Ashford with a partial report on the fifth day. The cable was true but it was also incomplete, and he did not examine too closely why the incompleteness felt deliberate rather than operational.

The partial report: the four names had been eliminated. The exfiltration window was not the source of the leak. The leak was older. Kovář had been under StB surveillance at least three weeks before the window opened, and the surveillance had characteristics that suggested the StB had known about CARDINAL for months, possibly longer. The leak was structural, not operational. Someone had given the StB not a name but a shape, and the StB had found Kovář by matching the shape to the space in their own records.

What Cole did not cable: the list. The seven names. LAZARUS. The false bottom in the desk drawer. The question mark. He kept these in his inside pocket and in his notebook and in the part of his mind where things sat before he understood them.

He also did not cable the growing, corrosive, unsubstantiated suspicion that the man who had sent him to Prague was the man he should have been investigating from the start.

---

The disinformation was harder to find than it should have been, which told Cole the StB had put their best people on Kovář, which told him Kovář had been important enough to justify the expenditure. This was, in its grim way, a compliment to Cole's recruitment. He had chosen well in Brno. The StB had confirmed it by the quality of the resources they had deployed to destroy his work.

He found it by cross-referencing Kovář's last six intelligence reports against the Service's master records. The process took two days and required him to cable London four times for specific file references, each time through Ashford's channel, each time receiving the reply within hours. Ashford was being helpful. Ashford was being exactly as helpful as a man in his position should be to an investigator in the field. Cole noted this and filed it in the place where he filed things that might mean nothing and might mean everything.

Three of the six reports contained subtle amendments. Dates shifted by small intervals: a meeting moved forward by two days, a troop rotation brought back by a week. Numbers adjusted: personnel figures rounded in directions that changed implications without changing plausibility. Nothing that would have been caught by a desk officer reviewing the intelligence in isolation. Nothing wholesale fabricated. Each amendment was small enough to be error and systematic enough to be design.

The StB had been running Kovář. Not as an agent, not as a double, but as a channel. They had identified him, placed him under surveillance, and instead of arresting him, which would have confirmed to the Service that the StB knew, they had allowed him to continue operating. They had fed selective disinformation back through him, adjusting the intelligence he passed so that it arrived in London subtly wrong. They were reading his intelligence as it left and they were shaping what it said on the way out.

Cole sat with this for a long time. Kovář had spent his last months operating inside a trap that was designed not to close but to remain open, because an open trap produces more intelligence about the enemy's methods than a closed one. The StB had been studying the Service through Kovář. Studying how it communicated, how it ran assets, how it responded to the intelligence it received. Kovář was not just an asset they had identified. He was a window they had opened into the architecture of British intelligence operations in Central Europe.

And Kovář had known. The missed contacts confirmed it. Kovář had felt the surveillance, not as evidence but as weight. He had missed two scheduled contacts not because he was frightened, not because he was cautious, but because he had calculated that making those contacts would confirm what the StB suspected and would expose the network that Cole had built around him. He was protecting Cole's network. He was protecting it with the only tool available to him, which was his own operational silence, and he had done this knowing what the silence would eventually cost.

Cole closed the file. He pushed it to the edge of the desk. He stood and went to the window and looked at the courtyard and at the building opposite and at the dark windows across from his. He was tired. Not the tiredness he was accustomed to. This was something else. He couldn't quite put his finger on what it was exactly. But it didn't sit well.

---

On the tram to the residency, the morning after finding the disinformation, Cole noticed a man. Thirty years spent assessing rooms and streets and railway platforms for the person who did not belong made his man stand out. The man was seated four rows ahead, facing forward. Forgettable face. Medium build. A coat that was neither new nor old. And shoes.

The shoes were good. This was the detail Cole caught and held, because in Prague in November 1983, good shoes were an anomaly. Shoes in Prague were functional. They were manufactured by state enterprises that prioritised durability over appearance and achieved neither. The shoes on the man four rows ahead were not Czech shoes. They were not Western shoes either, not obviously: no brand visible, no particular flair. They were simply good. Well-made. The shoes of a man who had access to something better than the state provided.

The man got off at the next stop. He did not look at Cole. Cole did not look at him. The tram continued. Cole filed the observation and let it sit. That evening, returning to the safe house after a long day at the residency, Cole unlocked the door to the flat and turned on the desk lamp. The window faced the courtyard. The building opposite was dark. As Cole watched, a light in a fourth-floor window went out.

He stood very still. He had not been watching the building opposite. He had turned on his lamp and glanced at the courtyard and seen the light extinguish itself with a timing that was either coincidence or discipline. Thirty seconds, perhaps. The interval between Cole's lamp coming on and the opposite light going out. Long enough to suggest no connection. Short enough to suggest otherwise.

Cole noted it. He said nothing in his next cable. He went to bed and did not sleep for a long time.

---

Three days later, Ashford redirected a cable.

This required explanation. Cole had sent a routine inquiry through the personal channel requesting access to the distribution logs for the Central Europe Desk's operational traffic during the period of Kovář's compromise. Distribution logs recorded every officer who received a copy of a given piece of intelligence, and they were maintained with the obsessive precision that characterised the Service's administrative apparatus, a precision that extended to recording who opened which door at what time but did not, apparently, extend to preventing the deaths of assets in the field.

The reply came not from the registry, as protocol dictated, but from Ashford himself. The distribution logs for the requested period were, Ashford explained, undergoing a routine audit and were temporarily unavailable. The audit had been scheduled for weeks. The timing was coincidental. Ashford would ensure Cole received the logs as soon as the audit was complete.

The explanation was plausible. Distribution log audits happened. They were scheduled in advance. The timing could be coincidental. Cole read the cable twice, folded it, and put it in his pocket with the two lists and the LAZARUS inquiry and the partial report and the growing archive of paper that he carried on his body the way another man might carry a weapon: close, accessible, and loaded.

He noted the redirection. He filed it. He said nothing. The temperature in the investigation changed by a degree that no instrument would have measured and that Cole felt in his spine.

There was also a second cable that day, separate from the redirection. Ashford inquiring after Cole's progress. Routine in phrasing, but the timing was wrong. Ashford had not asked for a progress update in the first five days. Now, on the day Cole had requested the distribution logs, Ashford wanted to know how things were going. The cable ended: "Trust Prague is not treating you too unkindly. Do let me know if you need anything. P.A." The warmth was characteristic. Ashford was warm the way a fireplace is warm: contained, but behind a grate. But the timing turned the warmth into something else, something that looked like warmth and functioned like pressure, and Cole replied with three sentences that said nothing and went to bed irritated in a way he could not entirely account for.

---

The matrix was nearly complete. Cole had built it over six days, working at the desk in the safe house with the lamp casting its circle and the courtyard dark beyond the window. It occupied four pages of his notebook, written in his small habitual hand, a system of abbreviations and cross-references so ingrained it was almost unconscious.

Every person who had touched Kovář's file in two years. Every access log. Every routing note. Every cable, every signal, every memo that referenced CARDINAL by designation or Kovář by implication. The four names Ashford had given him were eliminated. The exfiltration window was not the source. The leak was older and deeper, structural rather than operational, and it pointed not at a single moment of betrayal but at a sustained compromise of the architecture of the Service's Central European operations.

Someone had given the StB the shape. Not a name, not a specific piece of intelligence, but the patterns: how the Service ran assets, where the gaps were, what the architecture looked like from outside. The StB had taken that shape and laid it over their own records and found Kovář by the outline of what was missing, the negative space in the pattern that could only be explained by the presence of an asset they had not yet identified.

The four names were gone. The investigation had no floor. Cole sat at his desk in the circle of lamplight and looked at the four pages of his matrix and understood that he had eliminated the possible and was left with the impossible, which was that the leak did not originate in the operational handling of Kovář's exfiltration but in something older, deeper, and more structural than anyone in London had contemplated.

He opened his inside pocket. He took out both lists. Ashford's four names, all crossed off. Kovář's seven names, the seventh unknown, the question mark. He placed them side by side on the desk and looked at them and thought about LAZARUS and about Ashford and about the redirected cable and about good shoes on a tram in Prague.

Then he picked up his pencil and, on a fresh page of his notebook, wrote a name he had not written before: STOKES, G.R. It was not a name from either list. It was a name he had found buried in a routing note header in Kovář's 1972 recruitment file, a name that had been there for eleven years and had never been cross-referenced because no one had reason to cross-reference it, because Gerald Stokes had retired from the Service cleanly in 1979 after a decorated career and there was no flag on his file and no reason to look and no one had looked.

Cole looked at the name. He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed the notebook and put it in his pocket with the lists and the cables and went to bed and did not sleep.

The next morning, he sent a request through a channel that was not Ashford's. A back channel, older, less efficient, maintained by a retired signals officer in Vienna whom Cole had worked with in the seventies and who owed him a favour that had never been called in. The request was simple: the Vienna posting records for Gerald Stokes, 1977 to 1979. Everything the Service held. Everything the Station held. Everything that had been filed and forgotten.

Not through Ashford. Cole did not examine too closely why.
